Abstract: Remote sensing and computer vision technologies are increasingly leveraged for rapid
post-disaster building damage assessment, becoming a crucial and practical approach. In this context,
the accuracy of employing various AI models in pixel-level change detection methods is significantly
dependent on the consistency between pre- and post-disaster building images, particularly regarding
variations in resolution, viewing angle, and lighting conditions; in object-level feature recognition
methods, the low richness of semantic details of damaged buildings in images leads to a poor
detection accuracy. This paper proposes a novel method, OCD-BDA (Object-Level Change Detection
for Post-Disaster Building Damage Assessment), as an alternative to pixel-level change detection
and object-level feature recognition methods. Inspired by human cognitive processes, this method
incorporates three key steps: an efficient sample acquisition for object localization, labeling via
HGC (Hierarchical and Gaussian Clustering), and model training and prediction for classification.
Furthermore, this study establishes a change detection dataset based on Google Earth imagery of
regions in Hatay Province before and after the Turkish earthquake. This dataset is characterized
by pixel inconsistency and significant differences in photographic angles and lighting conditions
between pre- and post-disaster images, making it a valuable test dataset for other studies. As a result,
in the experiments of comparative generalization capabilities, OCD-BDA demonstrated a significant
improvement, achieving an accuracy of 71%, which is twice that of the second-ranking method.
Moreover, OCD-BDA exhibits superior performance in tasks with small sample amounts and rapid
training time. With only 1% of the training samples, it achieves a prediction accuracy exceeding that
of traditional transfer learning methods with 60% of samples. Additionally, it completes assessments
across a large disaster area (450 km²) with 93% accuracy in under 23 min.
Keywords: remote sensing; object-level change detection; post-earthquake; building damage
assessment; methodology
1. Introduction
Earthquakes are extremely destructive to building structures, often causing thousands
of houses to collapse or sustain severe damage. With the significant growth of urban
population and rapid urbanization, earthquakes have undoubtedly become the main threat
to buildings and other infrastructure in earthquake-prone areas [1]. Identifying and tallying
the damage in buildings after an earthquake is a significant challenge. Conventional
methods for detecting building damage have depended on field inspection techniques,
such as on-site surveys [2,3], vibration characteristic-based methods [4,5], finite-element-
model-updating methods [6,7], and the conventional short-time matrix pencil method [8].

Other studies have solved this problem through predictive simulation. For example,
Mardiyono et al. combined sensors with finite element analysis in order to simulate
building models, and then trained artificial neural networks predicting building damage
indices [9]. Chiauzzi et al. established a new relationship between Housner and EMS-98
earthquake intensities by using finite layer simulation, and applied a damage probability
matrix to estimate the damage level of residential buildings [ 10]. However, from the
perspective of practitioners, these methods are associated with high technical expertise,
and face difficulties in widespread application. For instance, after the Wenchuan earthquake
in 2008 and the Türkiye earthquake in 2023, rescue workers were forced to rely on labor-
intensive field surveys that were both inefficient and ineffective. This underscores the
urgent need for simpler and more effective technologies to address challenges at a regional
scale [11].
In contrast, Ogawa et al. relied solely on the visual assessment of a single remote
sensing image to provide a preliminary estimate of building damage in the entire region [12].
This highlights the advantages of image-based methods in building damage recognition,
which offer low costs, extensive coverage, and high efficiency, especially in the initial
response stage after large-scale disasters. This approach significantly advances resource
utilization and prediction speed compared to other methods. However, one drawback is
their reliance on the quality and integrity of the images, making it challenging to maintain
high accuracy.
Methods for post-earthquake building damage recognition based on remote sensing
images have been widely studied. As shown in Figure 1, these studies can be divided into
two categories: methods based on “Change Detection” and methods based on “Feature
Recognition”. The main difference between them is that change detection methods use
images taken before and after the disaster, while feature recognition methods rely solely on
images taken after the disaster. For the former, it is necessary to achieve more consistency
between multi-temporal images, such as the same angle and color. For the latter, the seman-
tic information in a single image is very limited. It is necessary to use high-precision remote
sensing images for training. On the other hand, objects in post-disaster remote sensing im-
ages exhibit complex semantic features. The remote sensing images of different regions and
architectural styles may yield varying prediction results under the same model, indicating
the need for an exceptionally large training dataset and a highly robust recognition model.
In other words, this implies that existing post-earthquake building damage assessment
methods face two common problems:
1. High requirements for the quality of remote sensing images;
2. Poor generalization and transfer capabilities.
High-quality remote sensing images are required for both change detection based
on multi-temporal data and feature detection methods based on single-temporal data.
The development of remote sensing image clarity is accompanied by the development of
image processing disciplines and they complement each other. Due to the characteristic
of regular updates in remote sensing images, pixel-level change detection methods have
always been a hotspot.
In the early stages of disciplinary development, the combination of threshold seg-
mentation methods and low-resolution remote sensing images was able to achieve decent
results in some large-scale rough tasks. For example, Yusuf et al. calculated the difference
between two images captured by Landsat-8 satellite before and after the 2001 India Gujarat
earthquake [13]. Similarly, Sugiyama et al. proposed an image difference algorithm based
on precise geographic locations in aerial images before and after events, which extracted
damaged areas by thresholding color differences [14].

However, with the further development of optical imaging technology, threshold
segmentation methods meet more refined task requirements. Therefore, researchers have
improved the single-feature threshold segmentation method to a multi-feature threshold
segmentation method based on this. For example, Janalipour et al. proposed a method that
utilizes post-earthquake high-resolution sharpening and pre-earthquake vector maps to
detect building damage in urban detection after earthquakes [15]. The overall accuracy of
this method was 76.36% when tested on the Bam earthquake dataset in Iran.
High-precision optical images and SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) images have
developed the pixel scale of remote sensing images to the decimeter level. Neural networks
have been able to excellently complete tasks at this pixel level since the birth of Alexnet [16].
However, these methods require that the pre- and post-disaster images exhibit a high degree
of consistency [17], and therefore, their reliability is only validated within high-quality
change detection datasets with similar semantics.
The feature recognition method could circumvent this issue, as it does not require
consistency between pre- and post-disaster images [ 18]. They typically achieve lighter
models and faster response times [ 19,20]. But this also means they need higher-pixel
remote sensing images [21]. It is evident that these methods still impose relatively stringent
requirements on image resolution and overall quality. However, obtaining high-quality
post-disaster remote sensing optical images of affected areas within a short timeframe
remains a significant challenge. Consequently, extensive research has been devoted to
addressing the above practical issues [22].
Multimodal fusion technology is recognized as a critical approach for overcoming
these challenges in change detection. Liu et al. introduced a cross-resolution differential
learning method that generates pixel difference maps for images of different resolutions
and subsequently fuses them to produce a comprehensive change map [23]. This approach
mitigates the adverse effects of combining images with varying resolutions on change detec-
tion accuracy. Comprehensive experiments conducted across four datasets demonstrated
the effectiveness of this method for detecting changes in images with different resolutions.
Zhang et al. employed assisted super-resolution (SR) learning to fuse multimodal data
for the high-resolution (HR) object detection of multi-scale targets, balancing detection
accuracy and computational cost [ 24]. Experimental results indicated that SuperYOLO
achieved an accuracy of 75.09% (measured by mAP50) on the widely used VEDAI RS
dataset, surpassing state-of-the-art models such as YOLOv5l, YOLOv5x, and RS-designed
YOLOrs by over 10%.

However, despite the promising results achieved by current methods on certain
datasets, they have shown limited effectiveness in practical applications, such as the Türkiye
and Noto earthquakes [ 11]. In other words, while these methods have shown notable
advancements in accuracy and robustness, they still face challenges in generalization.
The generalization capability of a model is influenced not only by its inherent per-
formance, but also by the quantity and quality of the training samples. Particularly in
the context of evaluating post-disaster buildings, variations in terrain and building styles
can significantly affect the model’s recognition accuracy. Therefore, enhancing generaliza-
tion through model performance improvements alone is challenging, while augmenting
it with additional training samples is both costly and impractical. Thus, it is essential to
develop methodologies that minimize costs and improve the efficiency of data acquisition,
annotation, and model training processes. Such approaches would facilitate the rapid
and cost-effective training of building damage assessment models following each disaster,
addressing the fundamental issue of limited generalization capability.
Based on this idea, this paper proposes an object-level post-earthquake building
damage assessment method called OCD-BDA (Object-Level Change Detection for Post-
Disaster Building Damage Assessment). OCD-BDA is mainly divided into three steps:
Step 1. Object-Level Pre- and Post-Disaster Data Pair Acquisition: Use YOLOv8 to
detect buildings from pre-disaster remote sensing images, and crop the corresponding
areas from post-disaster images based on coordinate mapping (as illustrated in Figure 1).
Here, the YOLOv8 model needs to be pre-trained through transfer learning using the xView
Building Dataset (xBD) (YOLOv8: https://github.com/ultralytics/ultralytics (accessed on
28 April 2024); xBD: http://xviewdataset.org/ (accessed on 15 April 2024)).
Step 2. Ultra-Efficient Labeling Method Based on Machine Learning Assistance: An
algorithm called HGC (Hierarchical and Gaussian Clustering) is proposed to perform
clustering operations on data pairs obtained from Step 1 and use manual assistance to
quickly correct classification errors.
Step 3. Object-level Post-Disaster Building Assessment based on Data Pairs and
Supervised Learning: An R50M2Net network is proposed to assess damaged buildings by
classifying them into three categories using labeled data pairs obtained after Steps 1 and 2.
2. Building Damage Assessment Criteria
Building damage is often assessed using grading systems [25]. Dong et al. summarized
various methods for determining the extent of building damage based on an extensive
review of case studies [26].
In this study, buildings that exhibit overall collapse, showing ruins or internal cross-
sections, are classified as Grade C. Buildings without significant upper damage are classified
as Grade A, while the remaining buildings are classified as Grade B (as shown in Figure 2).

3. Methodology of OCD-BDA
As shown in Figure 3, OCD-BDA consists of three main steps: acquisition, labeling,
and classification. In the first step, the detected buildings are transmitted to the downstream
detector in the form of pre- and post-disaster sample pairs. In the second step, sample
pairs are labeled with HGC and manually adjusted. HCG includes two built-in clustering
algorithms and is used to automatically generate pseudo-labels for sample pairs. In this
study, we found that the generated pseudo-labels are highly credible and require only min-
imal manual adjustment, reducing labeling costs by 95% compared to traditional methods.
In the last step, the proposed feature classifier, R50M2Net, is used to assess buildings based
on pre- and post-disaster data pairs. This network includes 49 convolutional layers and
one fully connected layer, followed by a multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers.
Compared to previous building damage detection methods, the method proposed in this
study demonstrates higher accuracy, efficiency, and generalization capability. Next, every
step in OCD-BDA is introduced in detail.
Figure 3. The overall architecture of OCD-BDA: YOLOv8 Lite for localization; HGC for labeling; and
R50M2Net for classification.
3.1. Step 1: YOLOv8-Based Pre- and Post-Disaster Data Pair Acquisition
In the field of building damage detection based on remote sensing images, YOLOv8
is often used for object-level tasks. However, distinguishing between building ruins and
vacant regions in post-disaster remote sensing images remains challenging [27]. Therefore,
YOLOv8 is only used to detect buildings from pre-disaster remote sensing images in
the OCD-BDA.
Additionally, the downstream detection head in the YOLOv8 network is rewritten to
ensure the lightweight nature and coherence of the network, extracting only key parameters
for sample pair collection. This significantly enhances the speed of network prediction.
Figure 4 shows the network structure of YOLOv8 Lite. The model extracts five feature
layers, uses the last three layers for training the detection head, and combines both Bbox
and cls loss functions. The output represents identified buildings with green bounding
boxes, and only the box coordinates need to be output. Finally, based on the output box
coordinates, the necessary data pairs are cropped from the pre- and post-disaster remote
sensing images.

3.2. Step 2: HGC-Based Rapid Labeling Method for Data Pairs
Dataset labeling is consistently the most time-consuming task in computer vision
applications. To address this challenge, this paper proposes a clustering-based auxiliary
labeling algorithm, referred to as HGC, which significantly accelerates the manual anno-
tation process. This approach is divided into three primary sub-steps: feature difference
extraction, pseudo-label generation, and manual adjustment. A detailed explanation of
each of these sub-steps is as follows.

of three texture features.

3.2.2. Pseudo-Label Generation
The HGC algorithm proposed in this study is based on Gaussian Mixture Models
(G) and Hierarchical Clustering (H). Figure 5 illustrates that the pre-trained YOLOv8 Lite
model was used to detect buildings from both pre- and post-disaster images. The number
of detected buildings is X and Y, respectively. Next, as introduced above, based on the
locations of the detected buildings in the pre-disaster images, corresponding data from the
post-disaster images were extracted using coordinate mapping. These were then combined
with the pre-disaster data to form X pairs of data.
Figure 5. Flowchart of HGC.
Then, based on the numerical relationship between the number of buildings X in
the pre-disaster images and Y in the post-disaster images for each area, we divided the
clustering process into three cases.
Case 1: When Y is less than one-third of X, we consider the area to have been severely
affected by the earthquake. First, we use H to perform an initial binary classification based
on the feature differences of the data pairs, labeling the cluster closest to the center as Grade
C. Next, we use G for a second clustering on the remaining data, labeling the cluster closest
to the center as Grade B and the other cluster as Grade A.
Case 2: Similarly, when Y is greater than two-thirds of X, we consider the area to have
been relatively lightly affected by the earthquake. First, we use H to perform an initial
binary classification based on the feature differences of the data pairs, labeling the cluster
closest to the center as Grade A. Next, we use G for a second clustering on the remaining
data, labeling the cluster closest to the center as Grade B and the other cluster as Grade C.
Case 3: For cases that do not fall into the above two categories, we first perform
clustering based on Hierarchical Clustering (H), dividing the data into two categories:
those close to the cluster center and those farther away. Next, we perform a second
clustering on each of these two categories using Gaussian Mixture Models (G). Based on

3.3. Step 3: R50M2Net-Based Assessment on Post-Disaster Buildings
R50M2Net primarily consists of two components: the feature extractor and the classi-
fier. In this study, ResNet50 is combined with a two-layer multi-layer perceptron (MLP).
Specifically, the ResNet50 feature extractor is retained, while its original classifier is replaced
with the MLP .
In general, the feature extractor in R50M2Net comprises 49 convolutional layers and
1 fully connected layer. A pair of images is processed through this network to generate a
pair of 1 × 1000 feature vectors. These vectors are then normalized following the processing
of their differences in the feature vector processing layer. The normalized results are fed
into the MLP classifier, which features a simple network structure. Figure 7 shows the
complete structure of the R50M2Net.
Among them, the feature vector length of 1000 is an important parameter. Resnet50
usually defaults to outputting vectors of length 2048, but we can also obtain vector outputs
of other lengths by modifying its convolution layers. It was found that the value of
this parameter is positively correlated with the final accuracy in the R50M2Net structure.
But when its value is higher than 1000, the growth trend of accuracy gradually slows down.
Therefore, for comprehensive consideration, we have selected a feature vector with a length
of 1000 as the output of the previous stage.

4. Generalization Capability Experiment
4.1. Overview of the Generalization Experiment
All the experiments in this study were conducted on a computer equipped with a 12th
Gen Intel® Core™ i7-12700H CPU (14 cores, base clock speed of 2.3 GHz) and an NVIDIA
GeForce RTX 4060 GPU with 8 GB of VRAM. The operating system used was Microsoft
Windows 11. The program was written in Python 3.9 using the PyCharm editor. For deep
learning tasks, we primarily utilized the PyTorch framework.
In the generalization capability experiments, four mainstream networks were tested
alongside OCD-BDA. The training datasets comprised xView and xView2, utilized in the
DIUx xView challenge, while the testing dataset was a self-constructed Turkish earthquake
dataset, focusing on the severely affected Hatay Province. Detailed descriptions of each
experimental subject and their specific information are provided in the subsequent sections.

4.3. Datasets
This study utilized two datasets: The first is the publicly available remote sensing
dataset, which was used for training. The second is a self-constructed dataset from the
Turkish earthquake, used for testing.
4.3.1. Dataset for Training: xView/xView2-Building Damage Class (xBD)
The large-scale remote sensing image dataset was specifically designed for building
damage assessment [29]. It contains high-resolution satellite images from various regions
and sensors, labeled for detecting and classifying the extent of building damage. The di-
versity of scenes and damage types covered in this dataset makes it highly valuable for
this research.
Notably, the dataset comprises 60 categories and has a resolution of 0.3 m, providing
image quality that surpasses that of most available satellite images, and the building
damage class was used in this study. The xView dataset, designed for object detection
tasks, was utilized for training both YOLOv8 and OCD-BDA, while the xView2 dataset,
intended for pixel-level semantic segmentation tasks, was employed for training several
other pixel-level change detection models.
4.3.2. Dataset for Testing: Turkey Earthquake—Antakya Region
This dataset is derived from satellite remote sensing images captured before and
after the 2023 Turkey–Syria earthquake. It includes pre- and post-disaster remote sensing
images from four regions within Hatay Province, obtained from Google Earth (as shown
in Figure 8). The pre-disaster images were taken around 22 December 2022, while the
post-disaster images were captured around 26 February 2023.
The images cover Antakya, Turkey, and its surrounding cities, spanning areas of
over 450 km 2 and impacting more than 300,000 inhabitants. The dataset comprises
7250 pairs of building samples, categorized into 3287 Grade C samples, 701 Grade B
samples, and 3262 Grade A samples.

variations led to considerable changes in texture features for the same building across the
two sets, causing the networks to mistakenly classify undamaged buildings as destroyed.
In contrast, YOLOv8.2 primarily identifies buildings classified as Grade A (undamaged
buildings). This limitation arises from the nature of feature recognition methods, which
differ from change detection approaches. Feature recognition requires target objects to
possess rich texture features, a criterion generally met by intact buildings. However,
buildings categorized as Grades B and C exhibit a significant loss of texture features due to
damage, making them challenging for YOLOv8.2 to recognize.
The proposed OCD-BDA integrates the strengths of both change detection and feature
recognition methods, allowing it to accurately identify targets across all damage grades.
As shown in Figure 9, while OCD-BDA occasionally misclassifies some Grade A buildings
as Grade C, it does not exhibit the pronounced class bias seen in Siam-ResNet, Siam-UNet,
and YOLOv8.2. However, OCD-BDA shows relatively weak performance in identifying
Grade B buildings, primarily due to the limited number of semi-damaged building samples
in the xView training dataset.
Furthermore, OCD-BDA’s strengths lie not only in its generalization capability but
also in its ability to label and train on entirely new datasets within a remarkably short
timeframe, enhancing its applicability across diverse scenarios. To validate this robust
capability, a comparative experiment was conducted between OCD-BDA and traditional
transfer learning methods based on YOLOv8.2, with detailed findings presented in the
following section.

5.3. Accuracy Comparison
A major challenge in transfer learning is determining the optimal number of samples
needed for fine-tuning to achieve high-accuracy predictions in a new task scenario [ 30].
As shown in Figure 11, when the training sample size is below 60%, the overall accuracy
(OA) of transfer learning remains under 65%, with most correctly detected samples classi-
fied as Grade A, representing buildings with little to no post-disaster damage. However,
these buildings are not the primary focus of rapid response efforts following a disaster.
In contrast, the OCD-BDA method achieves an OA exceeding 70% even with minimal
training samples, while the prediction results of traditional transfer learning methods are
notably poor. Although the accuracy gap between the two methods narrows as the training
sample size increases, OCD-BDA consistently maintains at least 50% higher accuracy than
traditional methods. When the training sample size reaches 10%, OCD-BDA’s accuracy
approaches 80%, and when the sample size exceeds 50%, its accuracy surpasses 90%.
Specifically, the performance of transfer learning methods in predicting samples
classified as Grades B and C is notably inadequate, with accuracy levels falling below
acceptable standards. In contrast, the OCD-BDA method reliably predicts Grade C samples,
representing buildings that have entirely collapsed post-disaster—a capability of high
importance for rapid disaster response. Moreover, as the training sample size grows, OCD-
BDA’s prediction accuracy for the more challenging Grade B samples exceeds 70%, while
transfer learning methods continue to struggle, with accuracy remaining below 40%.
Tables 4–7 present detailed comparative results, demonstrating that the OCD-BDA
method consistently outperforms transfer learning across all metrics. While transfer learn-
ing performs well in detecting Grade A buildings, its feature learning capacity for Grade B
and Grade C samples is limited. The accuracy of transfer learning methods only improves
significantly when the training sample size is sufficiently large, a typical characteristic of
traditional supervised learning approaches.
A noteworthy strength of the OCD-BDA method is its high performance in detecting
Grade C buildings, achieving over 80% accuracy even with a training sample size as low as
1%. However, the OCD-BDA method does encounter challenges in distinguishing between
Grade A and Grade B buildings, largely due to variations in the angle of building images
taken before and after the disaster, which can result in some Grade A buildings being
misclassified as Grade B. Nonetheless, this conservative approach proves advantageous
in rapid post-disaster response tasks, and as the training sample size increases, the rate of
misclassification decreases. Thus, we believe the OCD-BDA method is well suited for the
rapid assessment of building damage in post-disaster scenarios.

5.4. Efficiency Comparison
In addition to the accuracy advantages discussed, the lightweight design of OCD-
BDA also results in significant efficiency gains. Table 8 compares the efficiency of the
OCD-BDA method with traditional transfer learning approaches in the context of rapid
post-disaster response following the Turkey earthquake, with the assessment area detailed
in Section 4.3.2.
As illustrated in Figure 10, the time cost for transfer learning is primarily driven by
dataset labeling and neural network training for object detection. In transfer learning
tasks, data labeling must be performed on post-disaster remote sensing images, a time-
intensive process. Additionally, the neural networks used for detecting objects in remote
sensing images are complex, with numerous neurons and intricate architectures, resulting
in relatively long training times. In our comparative experiment, only 60% of the data were
labeled and trained. The labeling process alone for transfer learning took over 5 h, while
network training took approximately 7 min, bringing the total time cost to 320 min and
59 s.
In contrast, while the OCD-BDA method involves three time-incurring steps—extracting
building coordinates from pre-disaster images, performing HGC-based assisted data labeling,
and model training—the total time cost is only 6.97% of that required for transfer learning.
This efficiency is primarily due to the use of a pre-trained YOLOv8 model based on publicly
available datasets, which efficiently identifies buildings in pre-disaster images. Since this
technology is well established, there is no need to retrain the model for initial building
coordinate acquisition, saving substantial time, with the first step taking approximately 42 s.
Additionally , the second step, data labeling, requires only classification-level labeling rather
than bounding boxes for object detection as in transfer learning. This simpler labeling task,
further facilitated by HGC, significantly reduces the time required. As shown in Table 8,
the data labeling time for OCD-BDA is only 21 min and 36 s, far less than the 5 h required
for transfer learning. Finally , the R50M2Net, used for the classification task, has a simple
structure, resulting in minimal training time, taking only 5 s.

5.5. Discussion of Results
The accuracy improved by 50% and the efficiency increased by 1430% when using
OCD-BDA. This substantial enhancement in accuracy is particularly noteworthy given
the difficulties in recognizing Grade C samples in damage assessment tasks. Even human
annotators often struggle to accurately label object-level building damage on a single
pre-disaster image, which is a primary reason that pixel-level change detection using
multimodal remote sensing images is commonly adopted in disaster response tasks.
The design of OCD-BDA effectively addresses these challenges. By first using YOLOv8
Lite to locate targets in pre-disaster images, the method identifies easily recognizable
building samples, significantly simplifying object localization in post-disaster images.
Furthermore, we argue that OCD-BDA fundamentally addresses the issue of poor
model generalization by reducing dependence on pre-trained models and enabling efficient
labeling, training, and object detection in a remarkably short time. This achievement is
attributed to the following factors:
1. Training Capability with Minimal Samples: The OCD-BDA method demonstrates
the ability to accomplish tasks with only 1% of the training samples. Coupled with the
auxiliary labeling provided by the HGC method, it allows for the efficient completion of
data annotation tasks—traditionally the most time-consuming aspect of deep learning
projects—within a short timeframe.
2. Optimal Utilization of Existing Methods: Advances in computer vision have
produced highly effective neural networks for classification, object detection, and semantic
segmentation tasks. The OCD-BDA method addresses the challenge of indistinct target
features in post-disaster images by performing building detection solely on pre-disaster
images, thereby leveraging robust networks like YOLOv8. This approach transforms
complex object detection tasks into simpler classification tasks, making effective use of
networks such as ResNet and MLP .
3. Effectively Overcoming Challenges in Pixel-Level Change Detection: Pixel-level
change detection is significantly impacted by variations in remote sensing image quality,
capturing angles, and lighting conditions between pre- and post-disaster images. While
current research often focuses on increasing neural network complexity, improving feature
analysis, and incorporating multimodal data such as radar, the OCD-BDA method uses
object-level change detection to increase robustness and mitigate challenges associated
with pixel-level detection.
These strategies provide OCD-BDA with several unique advantages:
1. Intuitive Object-Level Results: OCD-BDA delivers object-level results, making out-
comes more interpretable and actionable.
2. Threshold-Free Predictions and High Robustness: OCD-BDA does not rely on threshold-
based decision-making for predictions, increasing its robustness across varying conditions.
3. Suitable for Early Disaster Response: OCD-BDA requires high-quality pre-disaster
images but imposes lower requirements on post-disaster images, making it well suited
to practical conditions in post-disaster response.
4. High Applicability: OCD-BDA minimizes the impact of training data quality on detec-
tion tasks, enabling rapid deployment across diverse, localized disaster scenarios.
5. Refined Assessment Capability: OCD-BDA effectively performs a three-class assess-
ment of building damage levels, providing detailed insights into disaster impact.
6. Conclusions
In this study , we present an object-level change detection framework for post-earthquake
building damage assessment, termed OCD-BDA. The OCD-BDA framework consists of
three main components: YOLOv8 Lite for localization and sample pair acquisition, HGC
for data labeling, and R50M2Net for final classification. This approach emulates human
learning strategies by dividing complex tasks into simpler steps, substantially reducing model
training costs and significantly improving overall efficiency . Additionally , by markedly
lowering the expenses associated with data acquisition, labeling, and neural network model

training, this method effectively addresses the issue of limited generalization in existing
approaches. Specifically , it enables the rapid creation, training, and evaluation of new datasets
tailored to each post-disaster area, thereby enhancing the adaptability and effectiveness of
the assessment process. The code is available at https://github.com/A YYCIAL/OCD-BDA/
tree/main.
Through comparative generalization experiments with four mainstream networks for
post-disaster building damage identification alongside OCD-BDA, we observed that the
recognition accuracy of OCD-BDA significantly exceeds that of existing networks. Each
network demonstrates specific recognition biases; for example, change detection networks
such as Siam-ResNet and Siam-UNet tend to classify targets as Grade C (destroyed build-
ings). This bias is primarily due to variations in shooting angles and lighting conditions
between pre- and post-disaster remote sensing images, which can cause network misclassi-
fications. Conversely, the feature recognition-based YOLOv8.2 favors identifying Grade
A targets (undamaged buildings), as feature recognition networks require rich semantic
and textural characteristics in the detected objects, while damaged buildings often lack
sufficient detail for accurate recognition. In contrast, OCD-BDA effectively recognizes
buildings across all damage grades without exhibiting such biases.
Furthermore, the strength of OCD-BDA lies not only in its generalization capability
but also in its ability to annotate and train on entirely new datasets within a very short
timeframe, greatly enhancing its applicability. In addition to generalization capability
experiments, this study conducted a comparative analysis between the traditional transfer
learning method and our OCD-BDA approach. Through this comparative study, we
found that OCD-BDA required only 1% of the training samples to achieve prediction
accuracy, surpassing that of supervised learning algorithms using 60% of the training
samples. Additionally, OCD-BDA completed a three-class damage assessment of post-
disaster buildings in just 22 min and 23 s, achieving 93% accuracy across an earthquake-
affected region spanning over 450 square kilometers and comprising 300,000 inhabitants.
Compared to traditional supervised learning methods, OCD-BDA improved accuracy by
50% and increased efficiency by 1430%.
In this study, we have focused solely on the performance of the OCD-BDA method
in the context of the Turkish earthquake. We believe that object-level change detection
holds greater potential for further exploration. Moving forward, we will investigate the
applicability of this method across various disaster scenarios, including tsunamis, typhoons,
and large-scale fires. Additionally, in post-disaster rapid response tasks, while quickly
assessing damaged buildings is crucial, the rapid evaluation of critical rescue lifelines is
equally important. Therefore, we will further study the applicability of the object-level
change detection method for post-disaster assessments of roads and bridges. We also
plan to collect data and establish a more diverse, application-level dataset that considers
inconsistencies between pre- and post-disaster images.
