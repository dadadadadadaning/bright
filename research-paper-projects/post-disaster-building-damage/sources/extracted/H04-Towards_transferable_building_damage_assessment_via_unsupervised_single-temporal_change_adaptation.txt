A B S T R A C T
Rapid and accurate assessment of building damage in sudden-onset disasters is crucial for effective humanitar-
ian assistance and disaster response. However, the occurrence of disasters is highly uncertain, e.g., unexpected
geographic location and hazards, which challenge the conventional building damage assessment model on
generalization and transferability. Unfortunately, there is little public literature on transferable building
damage assessment. This is because assessing building damage using pre- and post-disaster satellite images
is a complex, multi-temporal, and multi-task problem. It involves two main subtasks: building localization and
damage classification, which are non-trivial to handle with generic transfer learning approaches designed for
single-image and single-task problems. On the other hand, post-disaster training image availability in the target
domain remains an obstacle since these generic transfer learning methods require pre-/post-disaster image
pairs as target training images, resulting in a costly time window (period from obtaining post-event training
image to obtaining assessment results) in disaster response. In this paper, we present a single-temporal domain
adaptive semantic change detection framework, which frames domain adaptive building damage assessment
and only additionally requires target pre-disaster images for adaptation training. Our framework first presents a
decoupled task modeling via the equivalent form of prediction error expectations. This enables generic transfer
learning methods to be used for domain adaptive building damage assessment. To fundamentally overcome the
problem of post-disaster training image availability within our framework, we propose an unsupervised single-
temporal change adaptation (STCA) algorithm. The main idea is ‘‘damage is everywhere’’, which is motivated
by the fact that building damage is a change process driven by the disaster event. We leverage target pre-
disaster images and source post-disaster images to simulate such semantic change processes to provide training
data, fundamentally addressing the post-disaster training image availability issue and avoiding that costly time
window. The extensive experiments on global-scale and local-scale study areas suggest that our framework
allows most transfer learning approaches to work well on domain adaptive building damage assessment. Our
STCA achieves superior performance compared to other transfer learning approaches. More importantly, unlike
other approaches that rely on target pre/post-disaster images for adaptation, it requires no target post-disaster
training images. This nature significantly improves the availability of STCA in real-world disaster response for
the building damage assessment model.

on human life, property, and social stability ( Ge et al. , 2020). Build-
ing damage assessment based on multi-temporal high-resolution re-
mote sensing data is a safe, rapid, and effective way for humanitarian

(e.g., globally distributed geographic locations, stochastic occurrence,
and unknown hazards). Uncertain disaster events result in pre/post-
event Earth observation data with various data distributions, which
poses a significant challenge to model generalization ability.
Over the past decade, the paradigm of building damage assessment
has changed from expertise-driven feature engineering to data-driven
deep learning. Expertise knowledge-based approaches usually design
hand-crafted features for multisensor images (e.g. optical and SAR)
and then apply a classifier (e.g., support vector machines Boser et al. ,
1992; Cortes and Vapnik , 1995) or combine numerical simulation of
hazard intensity models ( Yokoya et al. , 2020 ; Moya et al. , 2021 ) to
obtain the assessment map for a single hazard or a specific disaster
event (Brunner et al., 2010; Tong et al. , 2012; Anniballe et al. , 2018).
Limited by expertise and prior information about disasters, developing
such a knowledge-based approach with expertise is time-consuming,
and this specialized method is difficult to generalize to other events
or hazards due to complex and diverse data distributions.
Fortunately, the rise of deep learning technology has changed this
situation recently. The key point is that deep learning models can
learn feature representation from raw data automatically. The deep
convolutional neural network (CNN) is a deep learning model for
image data. Thanks to its hierarchical representation learning capabil-
ity, CNN-based building damage assessment approaches ( Gupta et al. ,
2019; Durnov, 2020; Zheng et al., 2021b) can achieve better accuracy
and transferability than most hand-craft feature-based approaches if
massive training data is available and prior knowledge is insufficient
or hard to design good feature descriptors. The previous work ( Gupta
et al., 2019) designs a cascade object-based approach, which adopts a
fully convolutional network (FCN) to obtain each building mask and
then uses a CNN-based classifier to score the damage degree of each
building instance. This approach involves two CNN models; however,
due to inconsistent architectures, there is a knowledge gap problem,
i.e., two models are used for building localization and damage clas-
sification independently, ignoring the relationship between these two
subtasks at the modeling stage. To bridge this knowledge gap, Durnov
(2020) adopts pixel-based Siamese network approach. In particular,
they adopt two weight-shared UNets ( Ronneberger et al. , 2015 ) to
build localization and damage classification, with two-stage training
strategies that significantly outperform traditional object-based meth-
ods. However, the damage degree of each building instance is unique,
and the pixel-based approach always obtains multiple damage degrees
for different parts of a building instance, i.e., semantic inconsistency
problem. ChangeOS ( Zheng et al. , 2021b ), as a deep object-based
framework, is proposed to overcome this problem by deeply integrating
the advantages of object-based and pixel-based paradigms. ChangeOS
adopts a deep building localization network for object proposal and a
deep damage classification network for pixel-wise damage classifica-
tion, where they have a consistent architecture to share knowledge and
have a differentiable interaction via deep object features of the former
for semantic consistency. In addition to framework-level improvement,
specific network architecture-level improvement is also important. For
example, recent studies (Chen et al., 2022; Da et al., 2022) apply vision
Transformers (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2021) to enhance the
backbone network architecture in terms of long-range context modeling
to overcome the spatial locality of CNN. Although framework and ar-
chitecture improvements can somewhat enhance model generalization
ability, it is still difficult to overcome performance degeneration due to
image data distribution shifts ( Candela et al., 2009).
To overcome image data distribution shifts, transfer learning tech-
nologies, especially domain adaptation approaches (Tzeng et al., 2014;
Tsai et al. , 2018 ; Hoyer et al. , 2022 ), are widely used in generic
computer vision tasks, e.g., image classification, semantic segmenta-
tion. However, there are few studies on transfer learning for building
damage assessment. These existing studies (Liu et al., 2021a; Bouchard
et al. , 2022 ; Lin et al. , 2022 ) mainly adopt supervised fine-tuning,

which transfers a pre-trained model to target domains, e.g., apply-
ing ImageNet ( Krizhevsky et al. , 2012) pre-training or historical data
pre-training and then fine-tuning this pre-trained model to a desired
disaster event. Supervised fine-tuning requires target-domain annotated
data; however, massive annotated data is hard to collect due to limited
response time and post-disaster training data availability. It is difficult
to use these fine-tuning-based approaches in disaster response scenar-
ios. While there are potential solutions without any fine-tuning and the
assumption of post-disaster training data availability, e.g., combining
the unsupervised change detection model (Saha et al., 2019) with post-
event image-based damage assessment method ( Bai et al., 2017), they
are still hard to achieve promising results in unseen disaster events.
Generic unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) methods, as cur-
rent mainstream transfer learning technology, only extra need target-
domain images without any annotations to achieve domain alignment,
which has great potential for transferable building damage assessment
in disaster response scenarios. There are three typical ways to achieve
domain adaptation, that is, discrepancy-based approaches (Tzeng et al.,
2014; Long et al., 2015), adversarial learning ( Tsai et al., 2018; Wang
et al. , 2020 ), and self-training ( Hoyer et al. , 2022 ). These methods
essentially do the same thing, aligning inter-domain distributions. From
this perspective, applying UDA approaches to building damage assess-
ment is non-trivial since this task includes pre-disaster building local-
ization and post-disaster damage classification, which should consider
multi-temporal and multi-task feature distributions simultaneously. Re-
garding task modeling, generic UDA approaches mainly focus on a
single task, it is still an open problem how to align multi-temporal
multi-task feature distributions of the building damage assessment
models. Regarding time efficiency, these generic UDA approaches usu-
ally take several hours to train the model, even with many powerful
GPUs. The model training requires unlabeled bitemporal (pre-/post-
disaster) image pairs of the target domain. However, there is little
time to tune the assessment model after obtaining post-disaster im-
ages in these emergent scenarios, as illustrated in Fig. 1 . These two
problems create a huge obstacle between generic UDA approaches and
transferable building damage assessment.
In this paper, we present a single-temporal domain adaptive se-
mantic change detection framework for transferable building damage
assessment. To address the task modeling problem, we first formu-
late the domain adaptive building damage assessment problem and
break this multi-temporal multi-task domain adaptation problem into
two single-task domain adaptation subproblems. This decoupled task
modeling allows us to extend conventional generic UDA approaches
to building damage assessment. However, generic UDA approaches
are based on pre/post-disaster image pairs for model training, whose
availability is limited by the time window from acquiring post-disaster
images to obtaining disaster assessment results.
To free from the shackle of the time window in our framework,
we propose an unsupervised single-temporal change adaptation al-
gorithm that does not require target post-disaster images, enabling

2. Study area and data
The experimentals were conducted on a global-scale study site
as shown in Fig. 2 (a) and two local-scale study sites as shown in
Fig. 2(b) and (c) for statistical evaluation and real-world application
evaluation, respectively. The global-scale study site involves 19 disaster
events collected from the xView2 Building Damage Assessment (xBD)
dataset ( Gupta et al. , 2019 ). These disaster events have the global
spatial distribution and the temporal distribution that covers from 2011
to 2019, which are used for model training, transferability evalua-
tion, and ablation study. Two local-scale study sites, i.e., the 2023
Turkey-Syria Earthquake event and the 2023 Kalehe DRC Flooding
event, have different spatio-temporal distributions from the global-scale
study site. Thus, these two recent events were used to further verify
the transferability of our proposed approach in real-world application
scenarios.

2.1.2. Domain split for transferable building damage assessment
To conduct the transferability experiments, we need a domain split
to determine source-domain data and target-domain data, thus evaluat-
ing the transferability of selected building damage assessment models.
The original xBD dataset has an official dataset split of train (2799
pairs), test (933 pairs), holdout (933 pairs), and an extra split of
tier3 (6369 pairs), where train, test, and holdout splits have
the same ten disaster events and thus can be approximately regarded
as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d). The tier3 split has
nine other disaster events with different spatio-temporal distributions
from the other three splits. Intuitively, different event sets always
have different data distributions due to different geographical locations
and event times. Therefore, we use tier3 split as the source-domain
data and the train/test/holdout splits as the target-domain data
for cross-domain evaluation of building damage assessment. The label
of target-domain data is unavailable unless for evaluation. For trans-
ferable building damage assessment, labeled tier3 and unlabeled
train are used for model training; test and holdout are used
for evaluation and analysis, where their labels are available only for
evaluation. This protocol is illustrated in Fig. 3. The details of the
disaster event for the domain split are listed in Table 1.
2.2. Local-scale study areas
2.2.1. Turkey–Syria earthquake (2023)
The earthquake occurred on February 6, 2023, in the Turkish
province of Kahramanmaras, approximately 23 km east of Nurdagi
in the Gaziantep province near the Syrian border. It registered a
devastating magnitude of 7.8 M w, making it the deadliest earthquake
worldwide in over a decade. The earthquake caused widespread dam-
age across southeastern Turkey and northern Syria. Entire city blocks
and thousands of buildings were reduced to rubble, including hospitals,
schools, apartments, and historic sites. Aftershocks above 6.0 Mw struck
the region in the days following, further damaging structures.
The pre/post-disaster images were collected from WorldView-2,
WorldView-3, and GeoEye-1 satellites. All related publicly available
satellite images from Maxar Open Data Program (https://www.maxar.
com/open-data/turkey-earthquake-2023) are shown in Fig. 2(b) to
present their spatial covering. For some missing pre-disaster images,
we use Bing satellite images as supplementary. To obtain ground truth
for model evaluation, we leverage Microsoft’s Turkey Building Damage
Assessment product (Robinson et al., 2023) and expert correction to
obtain more accurate assessment labels.
2.2.2. Kalehe DRC flooding (2023)
The flooding occurred in the town of Kalehe in South Kivu province
in eastern DRC, one of the country’s deadliest disasters in recent
history. The flooding began in early January 2023 after heavy rains
caused Lake Kivu overflowing its banks. Flooding and damage contin-
ued through January and February. Thousands of homes, businesses,
fields, and infrastructure were damaged or destroyed by floodwa-
ters and landslides. The heavy rainfall that led to the flooding was
attributed to an unusually wet rainy season.

3.2.2. Unsupervised single-temporal change adaptation
To improve the transferability of the building damage assessment
model and avoid time window problem, we propose the unsuper-
vised single-temporal change adaptation (STCA) algorithm for domain
adaptive building damage assessment, as illustrated in Fig. 5 (b). Fun-
damentally differing from conventional generic UDA approaches that
require source and target bitemporal pre/post-disaster image pairs to
align source and target semantic change feature distributions, STCA
only needs target single-temporal pre-disaster images and source single-
temporal post-disaster images to achieve that alignment, thereby en-
abling the model to adapt new disaster events at any time.
The main idea of STCA is ‘‘damage is everywhere’’, as illustrated
in Fig. 6. This idea follows an insight that the building damage is a
change process driven by the disaster event. For each pixel location,
the conventional bitemporal damage sample consists of a regular pixel
and a damaged pixel. The damage label is the state transition of the
building caused by the disaster event, e.g., from regular to destroyed.
This pixel pair is generally defined on the identical geographic loca-
tion and the same building instance. Thus, learning to recognize the
building damage is essentially learning one-to-many semantic change
representation at the pixel level. However, consistent geographic loca-
tions and building instances are not mandatory to learn such pixelwise
semantic change representation, since the pixel values encode neither
geographic location nor object instance information. This means that
the original one-to-one damage sample construction, constrained by the
positional consistency condition ( Zheng et al., 2021a), can be relaxed.
We can construct bitemporal building damage samples from different

4. Experiments
4.1. Experimental settings
4.1.1. Implementation details
At each stage, all the models were trained for 40k iterations using
stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a ‘‘poly’’( 𝛾 = 0 .9) learning
rate schedule, where the initial learning rate was set to 0.03 for CNN
backbone and 6e−5 for Transformer backbone, respectively. The weight
decay was set to 0.0001 for CNN backbone and 0.01 for Transformer
backbone. The momentum of SGD was set to 0.9. The total batch
size is 16. For training data augmentations, we adopt random flip,
90◦ ⋅ 𝑘 rotation, scale jitter, and random crop of 512 × 512. All bench-
marked models were trained for two stages, i.e., supervised bitemporal
pre-training and unsupervised domain adaptation. For unsupervised
domain adaptation, we use the tier3 split with labels as source
domain data and the train split without labels as target domain data.
The test split is used to tune the hyperparameter and the holdout
split is used to evaluate the final performance. The settings for each
model are the same for a fair comparison.

4.2. Benchmark methods
To evaluate the proposed framework and STCA algorithm, we first
extend several classical and state-of-the-art transfer learning methods
on ChangeOS (Zheng et al., 2021b) with our proposed framework
to domain adaptive building damage assessment. Each method can
be seen as a composite of our proposed framework, the ChangeOS
model, and their domain adaptation method. These domain adaptation
methods are briefly introduced as follows:
1. Deep domain confusion (DDC) (Tzeng et al., 2014) is a
discrepancy-based deep domain adaptation method, which ex-
traly adds a single adaptation layer and a discrepancy loss,
i.e., minimizing single-kernel maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD) distance., to learn the domain-independent feature rep-
resentation.
2. Deep adaptation network (DAN) (Long et al., 2015) is a
discrepancy-based deep domain adaptation method, which fur-
ther adopts multi-layer adaptation and multi-kernel MMD dis-
tance as the discrepancy loss.
3. AdaptSeg (Tsai et al., 2018) is a domain adaptation method
based on deep adversarial learning, where a segmentation net-
work is seen as a generator, and a domain discriminator is used
to determine which domain each pixel belongs to. Thus, this
method can be used to adapt structured output space.
4. Transferable normalization (TransNorm) (Wang et al., 2019) im-
proves the transferability of deep networks via domain-specific
normalization. They claim that the BN statistics encode domain
information; therefore, normalization should be domain-aware
and responsible for domain discrepancy.
5. Fine-grained adversarial learning (FADA) (Wang et al., 2020) is a
domain adaptation method based on deep adversarial learning,
where its discriminator plays not only a domain distinguisher
but also a class-level domain classifier. Thus, it can achieve
fine-grained feature alignment.
6. Fourier domain adaptation (FDA) (Yang and Soatto, 2020) is
a data transformation-based domain adaptation method, which
reduces the domain discrepancy via replacing the low-level fre-
quencies of the target images into the source images before
training. This results in many source images in target styles;
thereby, this method achieves domain alignment by training on
these transformed source labeled images.

based damage assessment method
As presented in Table 2, compared with 𝑓dam of ChangeOS + PCC,

i.e., 𝑓dam of ChangeOS. This is because change detection errors of PCC
and unsupervised change detection methods are still large and usually
hard to achieve satisfactory performance in global disaster scenarios,
compared to supervised methods. These errors accumulate on the al-

performance is limited by the performance of the unsupervised change
detector and source-only post-event image-based damage assessment
method, suffering from quadratic error accumulation problem. Com-
pared to this method, our STCA is more promising and has superior
performance because it does not require any additional models, thus
avoiding the quadratic error accumulation problem.

4.4. Method analysis
Our whole framework includes decoupled task modeling, supervised
bitemporal pre-training, and STCA. STCA is the only part involved in
hyperparameter selection, and the other two keep the same settings for
all comparisons. Therefore, in this sub-section, we discussed how these
key hyperparameters impact the cross-domain performance in transfer-
able building damage assessment. The main results are summarized in
Fig. 7.
4.4.1. Threshold for target building prediction
In STCA, we need a binarized target building mask to indicate
which feature points are selected. This binarization is implemented
by thresholding; therefore, the threshold value is essential. Since we
use sigmoid to obtain the prediction during training and inference, a
threshold of 0.5 is an ideal choice if the estimation is unbiased. The
results of Table 3 also suggest that this threshold of 0.5 is optimal. In
practice, a lower threshold, like 0.3, brings more noisy predictions, and
these errors are accumulated twice via constructing pseudo-bitemporal
feature pairs, thus harming the adaptation process. Increasing the
threshold to 0.7 reduces the number of predictions but leads to higher
confidence. As a result, there is a significant decrease in the quantity
of pseudo-bitemporal damage samples. From this result, we can deduce
that the quantity of pseudo-bitemporal damage samples is relatively
more critical than their quality.

4.4.3. Loss function for change adaptation
The change adaptation is achieved by minimizing the classification
loss of pseudo-bitemporal damage samples. We choose two typical
loss functions as 𝓁dam for change adaptation, respectively, i.e., cross-
entropy and lovasz-softmax (Berman et al., 2018). From Fig. 7(c), we
can observe that STCA variants based on these two loss functions both
significantly outperform the baseline result (38.1%) by a large margin.
This confirms that the decisive factor for STCA to work well is not the
specific form of the loss function but the pseudo-bitemporal damage
samples. Table 5 presents that better loss function can bring more
gains to the final performance of STCA. This is because the cross-
entropy is well-known to suffer from the class-imbalanced problem,
while lovasz-softmax loss provides a tractable surrogate to optimize
the intersection-over-union that is very sensitive to class-imbalanced
predictions, thus significantly improving the recognition performance
of minor and major damage classes. This advantage can be successfully
inherited by STCA.

4.4.5. Discussion: model-invariant and hazard intensity model-based sam-
ple construction
The pseudo-bitemporal damage sample construction of STCA be-
longs to a model-invariant sample construction, which is independent
of hazard types. This sample construction can also be guided by hazard
intensity models ( Yokoya et al., 2020; Moya et al. , 2021). Their main
differences lie in interpretability and construction. Our model-invariant
pseudo-bitemporal damage sample construction focuses on providing
many effective pseudo-bitemporal damage samples at a low construc-
tion cost. Our samples are used to train deep neural networks, thus we
focus on a construction strategy with scalability. The model-based dam-
age sample construction will produce interpretable damage samples
but with higher construction costs. These extra costs are mainly from
hazard-specific mechanism/physical models and necessary metadata
to support the mechanism/physical models. These two strategies have
their suitable scenarios. Thanks to the flexibility of our framework,
they can seamlessly switch each other based on data (disaster metadata
required by hazard intensity model, etc.) availability when a disaster
event occurs.
4.5. Application on local-scale study areas
To demonstrate the effectiveness of our framework on real-world
disaster events, we conducted transferable building damage assessment
applications on three disaster events in 2023, that is, Turkey-Syria
earthquake, Kalehe floods in the Democratic Republic of the Congo,
and Maui-Hawaii fire, as case studies.

resampled them to align the WorldView-3. No extra image enhance-

much time as possible.

5. Conclusion
We present a single-temporal domain adaptive semantic change
detection framework, which frames domain adaptive building damage
assessment and only needs pre-disaster images as training data. This
framework bridges the gap between generic UDA approaches and do-
main adaptive building damage assessment. Besides, we propose an
unsupervised single-temporal change adaptation algorithm to funda-
mentally address the post-disaster training image availability issue,
thereby avoiding the time-window problem in the disaster response.
The numerous experiments and three instances of real-world applica-
tion confirm that our framework enables most UDA approaches to work
well on domain adaptive building damage assessment. Moreover, our
STCA, which utilizes only target pre-disaster images, improves over
other UDA approaches that still require target pre/post-disaster images
by a large margin. These results confirm that the method designed
based on the disaster change process is more suitable for real-world dis-
aster response application scenarios in a simple yet effective way. STCA
is a hazard-intensity model-invariant method; its interpretability needs
to be further improved by introducing knowledgeable hazard-intensity
models and numerical simulation in the future.
