ABSTRACT
A timely understanding of the scale and distribution of damage to infrastructure such as buildings 
and dwellings following disasters is crucial for efficient and effective response and recovery. 
Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is a valuable tool for remotely assessing damage, and when 
coupled with Machine Learning (ML) has the potential to safely and effectively map damage at an 
individual building level. For practical disaster response, SAR ML models must generalise to new 
disaster events beyond their training data. Model transferability to new disaster events in 
differing contexts has been untested with medium resolution SAR. Therefore, this study evaluates 
transferability of medium resolution SAR ML models using Sentinel-1 coherence and intensity for 
mapping damage across 11 disaster events of various types (including earthquakes, hurricanes, 
wildfires, tsunamis and volcanic eruptions) across different global locations. Models were trained 
and tested under three scenarios: a reference scenario requiring no transferability, and two 
transferability scenarios utilising event-based cross-validation: (a) a global scenario and (b) a fire- 
specific scenario. Model performance decreased substantially under the global transferability 
scenario compared to the reference scenario, indicating the models struggled to adapt to varying 
disaster characteristics and geographic contexts. The fire specific scenario outperformed the 
disaster-agnostic global scenario, suggesting that further research may benefit from focusing on 
disaster-type specific models. Although performance across all scenarios was inconsistent, several 
disaster events saw positive performance, particularly in the fire-specific scenario. Further 
research is necessary before models should be operationally utilised for disaster response.
1. Introduction
Natural Disasters have killed more than 5,000,000 people since the year 2000 [ 1 ], and affect nearly 200,000,000 people each year 
[ 2 ]. Natural disasters result in acute effects, such as infrastructure damage and building collapse which demand timely response and 
intervention, as-well as long-term and secondary effects including mass forced displacement [ 3 ], water and food insecurity and famine 
[ 4 ], disease outbreak [ 5 ] and short and long term economic damage [ 6 ]. A timely understanding of the scale and spatial distribution of 
damage is crucial for providing humanitarian support and coordinating post-disaster recovery efforts [ 7 – 9 ]. The primary method of 
collecting information about disaster impact and humanitarian needs is typically on ground surveys and door-to-door questionnaires, 
which, due to the scale of events and complexities of safety and site accessibility, can result in significant delays and can lack in 
repeatable or uniform assessment methods across the extent of the disaster event [ 10 , 11 ]. Inconsistent datasets can also lead to gaps in

Satellite remote sensing is increasingly being utilised for post-disaster damage assessment [ 10 ], with organisations such as the 
United Nations Satellite Centre and the Copernicus Emergency Management Service utilising Very High Resolution (VHR) optical 
imagery to visually assess the extent and distribution of damage, and then providing these damage assessments for humanitarian 
organisations to utilise in planning and prioritising disaster response [ 12 , 13 ]. While faster than on-ground assessment, this can be 
significantly delayed by the time taken to capture a suitable image, which is often limited due to cloud cover or smoke [ 14 ].
Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active form of remote sensing, particularly useful for rapid disaster response as it is not 
interrupted by cloud cover or smoke [ 15 ], and through interferometry, millimetre changes in height can be detected [ 16 ]. Despite 
these advantages, medium-resolution SAR, such as from Sentinel-1, faces limitations for precise, building-level damage detection due 
to factors such as resolution, speckle and shadow [ 15 ]. While a variety of change detection techniques are commonly used for 
interferometric coherence and intensity-based SAR damage assessment, Machine Learning (ML) is relatively untested [ 17 , 18 ]. Pre -
vious attempts at using ML and medium resolution SAR have resulted in a wide range of model performance, and have generally 
consisted of small case studies focusing on one or two events. A gap exists in how these models are validated, and limited testing has 
been completed at a large scale.
For a ML algorithm to be useful for rapid disaster assessment, it must be transferable to new disaster events beyond its training 
context [ 19 ]. Existing SAR ML studies have largely overlooked generalisability testing, with most training and testing conducted on 
ground truth data from the same disaster event [ 20 – 23 ], which fails to represent the differing disaster characteristics and geographic 
environments that these models must adapt to in practice. Studies that have tested transferability reported significant performance loss 
compared to those that did not [ 24 – 26 ]. To the best of the authors' knowledge, no transferability testing has been conducted for 
medium-resolution SAR intensity and coherence ML disaster damage mapping. Consequently, previous studies that failed to assess 
transferability may be unreliable in practice.
To understand the feasibility of a SAR ML damage detecting model, the limitations of the model adaptability and transferability 
must be appropriately understood. This study, therefore, defines two research questions: Firstly, to what extent does the performance 
of SAR building damage ML classification models decline when transferring to unseen disaster events across different disaster types 
and geographic regions? Secondly, does a disaster-specific model outperform a disaster-agnostic model?
To answer these questions, Sentinel-1 SAR features are used with ground-truth building damage labels from multiple disaster 
events to build ML models under three different training and testing scenarios, namely: 1) a reference scenario, acting as an idealised 
baseline that does not reflect realistic generalisability requirements, 2) a global transferability scenario, and 3) a wildfire-specific 
scenario.
2. Background
2.1. Introduction to Sentinel-1 SAR data
Sentinel-1 operates a C-band SAR instrument, which, in Interferometric Wide-Swath (IW) mode, captures both intensity and phase 
information. Intensity information is a measure of the fraction of incident radar energy reflected to the sensor, often called backscatter 
[ 27 ]. The phase of the returning radar signal is also captured, where one complete cycle of phase corresponds to one full wavelength of 
the sensor. With two measurements over time, a phase difference can be captured and therefore displacement can be measured, and is 
referred to as SAR interferometry or inSAR [ 15 ].
The Level-1 Single-Look Complex (SLC) Sentinel-1 SAR image has a spatial resolution of approximately 5 × 20 m due to the slant 
incident angle and is highly noisy. ‘Speckle ’ , particularly, is a salt-and-pepper like noise effect caused by constructive or destructive 
interference of the SAR signal within a single resolution cell, which can obscure and complicate detection of small features. Therefore, 
some level of averaging or ‘multilooking ’ is typically applied to achieve square pixels, reduce noise, and improve radiometric accuracy 
[ 15 ]. Sentinel-1 captures information in a variety of polarisations, of which the ‘VV ’ polarisation has been identified as being the most 
suitable for damage detection [ 22 ], meaning that the radar signal is both transmitted and received in a vertical polarisation.
2.2. Statistical damage assessment techniques
Many change detection methods have been proposed to assess damage using SAR, with the most successful methods incorporating 
both phase and intensity information [ 17 ].
Phase information is typically incorporated into SAR damage assessment through coherence, calculated from two complex images 
using a moving cross-correlation window [ 15 ]. High coherence indicates unchanged areas, while low coherence suggests change, 
making it useful for detecting disaster-related damage [ 28 ]. However, coherence is also naturally low in vegetation, water, and other 
dynamic surfaces [ 15 ], leading to false positives. To address this, many approaches compare pre-event coherence (normal variability) 
with co-event coherence (event-related change) to better isolate damage [ 17 ]. A variety of methods of comparing the two coherence 
images exist to produce a ‘proxy ’ for damage, including a ratio comprising the normalised difference of pre and co-event coherence 
[ 20 ]. Others [ 29 , 30 ] calibrate the pre-event image to the co-event image through histogram matching, and then subtract the pre-event 
image from the co-event image.
The application of these metrics at the building level faces inherent resolution constraints. Natsuaki et al. [ 31 ] suggests that for 
building level damaged to be detected reliably, the damaged building must be the size of the window used for coherence calculation. 
Given that Sentinel-1 has an effective square pixel size of roughly 14m (with a coherence window even larger), detecting damage at an

2.4. The importance of model transferability and robust performance evaluation
For a damage-assessment algorithm to be useful, it is essential that the algorithm is applicable to a new disaster scene outside of the 
context of the data used to train the model. Therefore, accuracy statistics should be calculated using disaster events entirely separate 
from the events in the training data [ 19 ]. Data utilised for model validation and error calculation should be independent from the data 
used to train the model, and generally, this is satisfied through randomly splitting data into train and test groups, however for spatial 
data, this generally underestimates predictive errors for regions outside of the sampled region [ 41 ] Ploton et al. [ 42 ] established that 
(for forest biomass estimation) almost all predictive power was lost when using a spatially segmented validation method as compared 
to a random split, indicating that almost all the model predictive power was due to local spatial autocorrelation in the input datasets. 
This particularly raises concerns with studies such as Xie et al. [ 23 ], where geographic coordinates are an input and spatial validation 
techniques aren't used, and therefore the model could effectively simply be learning interpolation. Robustness in testing is even 
more-so important for a disaster-assessment algorithm, where the algorithm will also need to be adaptable to disaster events of 
different magnitudes and scales, as well as to different landscapes and urban settings. The interferometric and backscattering char -
acteristics of a destroyed structure will likely vary significantly based on the changing setting and characteristics of that structure, 
which varies geographically, as shown in Fig. 1 .
Most models reviewed failed to test transferability, and those that did, suffered significant performance losses. Gokon et al. [ 24 ], 
utilising pre and post-event TerraSAR-X intensity, and a decision tree to classify damage to buildings after the 2011 Tohoku earthquake 
and tsunami, achieved reasonable accuracy with a three class classification of 67.4%, however when the model was transferred to a 
new region, the accuracy was reduced to 58.7%. Wieland, Liu and Yamazaki [ 26 ] chose the same event, however utilised TerraSAR-X 
as-well as ALOS PALSAR intensity images, pre and post event, on a binary classification task of ‘change ’ and ‘no change ’ , using a 
Support Vector Machine. This model achieved a Macro F1 score of 85%, however when trained on a local subset of the training data to 
test transferability, the score reduced to 78%, with the optimal number features selected also changing. Macchiarulo et al. [ 25 ], using 
post-event Capella intensity imagery with a RF on a series of earthquake events, utilised event-based cross validation, thoroughly 
testing the transferability and applicability to new earthquakes. The model achieved a binary accuracy between 73% and 86% when

3. Methods
This study employed a Random Forest (RF) classifier to detect building damage from Sentinel-1 SAR data across multiple disaster 
types, including earthquakes, wildfires, hurricanes, tsunamis, and volcanic eruptions, evaluating how model performance changed 
under different transferability testing scenarios. Ground truth building damage labels were sourced from the xBD dataset and the 
Copernicus Emergency Management Service. SAR data from Sentinel-1 was pre-processed to extract features such as coherence and 
intensity, as-well as derived image texture metrics. Three testing scenarios were applied: a reference scenario, a global scenario, and a 
wildfire-specific scenario. Model performance was evaluated using Overall Accuracy (OA) and Macro F1-Score to address class im -
balances between damaged and undamaged buildings, as-well as Precision-Recall Area Under Curve (PR-AUC) to understand model 
discriminative power. Subsequent sections provide further details on the disaster types and study area, ground truth dataset selection, 
methods for SAR image processing, testing scenarios, ML algorithm implementation and model evaluation.
3.1. Disaster types and study area
Eleven disaster events, displayed in Fig. 2 below, were chosen to be utilised for model training and evaluation. Disaster events were 
included from different geographic contexts, including eight different countries over three continents, enabling testing of model 
transferability and adaptability to different natural and built environmental contexts.

A variety of disaster types were included in the study, including earthquakes, wildfires, hurricanes, a tsunami, and a volcanic

allowing for the testing of the algorithm's transferability across different disaster contexts. Disaster events were included on the basis

selected disaster types. Inundation-driven damage such as floods fundamentally alter the SAR signal through water-related absorption 
and specular reflection [ 15 ]. Because flood impacts produce a different set of scattering behaviours that are not directly comparable to 
collapse-type damage, flood events were excluded from this study.
3.2. Datasets
3.2.1. Ground truth building damage labels
3.2.1.1. Ground truth dataset selection. Ground truth data was sourced from the xBD dataset [ 43 ] and the Copernicus Emergency 
Management Service (CEMS) Rapid Mapping Archive [ 12 ]. The xBD dataset, produced by the United States Defence Innovation Unit, 
was originally designed for a VHR optical satellite imagery machine learning challenge, however the labels have been provided as open 
data with georeferencing information for alternative uses. The xBD dataset has been reviewed by a variety of experts including the 
California Air National Guard and the United States Federal Emergency Management Agency, and can be considered a very reliable 
source of ground truth building damage assessment, with an estimated error rate of less than 2-3% [ 43 ]. The CEMS Rapid Mapping 
Archive, produced by a programme of the European Union, are damage grading products released for active disaster response, digitised 
manually from very high resolution optical satellite imagery with an estimated thematic accuracy of 85% [ 44 ] and released as open 
data [ 12 ]. From these two sources, a total of 205,731 ground truth building polygons were gathered.
For this study, the polygons from the ground truth dataset were used for SAR feature extraction, as discussed later in section 2.3.3.2. 
Building polygons from the CEMS included polygons derived from globally available datasets such as OpenStreetMap, meaning in the 
practical application of this method, the required building footprints would be readily accessible from either OpenStreetMap or other 
available datasets such as the Microsoft Building Dataset [ 45 ] without preprocessing delays.
3.2.1.2. Label classification. Building damage gradings were reclassified to either ‘Damaged’, meaning the partial or complete collapse 
of walls, roof, or the complete building, or ‘Undamaged’, meaning minor missing building elements, cracks or a completely unaffected 
building. Based upon the respective damage grading scales and visual analysis of the xBD dataset and Copernicus products, the input 
datasets were aligned as per the below Table 2 .
for the xBD dataset, ‘Major-damage’ or above was considered as damaged, and for the CEMS products, ‘moderately damaged’ or 
higher was considered as damaged. The resulting ground truth dataset includes a significant class imbalance, with most buildings 
classified as undamaged across the eleven disaster events, as demonstrated in Fig. 3 .
3.2.2. SAR data
3.2.2.1. SAR image selection and acquisition. For each disaster event, the three (two pre-event, one post-event) timeliest Sentinel-1 
Interferometric Wide Swath Single-Look-Complex (SLC) images of consistent orbit with both VV and VH polarisations and with 
coverage of the disaster area was acquired, sourced from Alaska Satellite Facility [ 46 ]. Although the acquisition repeat time was 
generally spaced evenly at 6 or 12 days, this was not always the case, as shown in Table 3 . This variation in acquisition repeat time was 
deemed acceptable because it reflects the realistic acquisition consistency and frequency that may exist when a real disaster occurs.

3.3. Testing scenarios
Three different testing scenarios were utilised for the assessment of transferability and are outlined below.
3.3.1. Scenario 1 - reference scenario
The reference scenario acts as an idealised baseline of previous algorithm performance under in-domain testing scenarios. For each 
disaster event, the labelled data was split randomly into 80% training and 20% testing data. A model was then built and evaluated for 
each disaster event. This represents situations where a model has been trained and tested on local data from the same disaster event, 
which is the most favourable conditions for performance but is not representative of real-world generalisation requirements.
3.3.2. Scenario 2 - global scenario
The global scenario evaluates cross-event generalisation with no event-specific training data. Utilising an event-based ‘leave-one- 
out ’ cross validation training/testing split, described in Fig. 7 below, where, for every disaster event, an algorithm was trained on all 
data from all other disaster events, and then tested on the left-out disaster event.
This represents a model as if it were applied to map building damage rapidly following the disaster, without access to any local 
training data from that specific disaster event. The global scenario specifically evaluates the operational case where a model leverages 
all available historical disaster data to maximise training samples, accepting that training from diverse disaster types may introduce 
conflicting signals between different damage mechanisms.
3.3.3. Scenario 3 - wildfire specific scenario
The wildfire specific scenario assesses whether a wildfire-specific model improves generalisation relative to a multi-hazard model. 
It employs a ‘leave-one-out ’ cross validation training/testing split, however the model is only trained and tested on the wildfire disaster 
events. This represents a hazard-specific wildfire model as if it were applied to map building damage due to a wildfire rapidly following 
the disaster, without access to any local training data from that specific event. Wildfires were selected for this scenario as there were 
four disaster events in the ground truth damage labels, enabling use of ‘leave-one-out ’ testing.

3.4. Machine learning model selection and implementation
A Random Forest (RF) classifier was utilised for its computational efficiency and competitive performance, as-well as its robust 
handling of high dimensional data [ 50 ]. The RF is also well established in SAR damage mapping literature [ 23 , 25 , 37 , 38 , 40 ] and offers 
feature explainability metrics [ 51 ], allowing for interpretation and comparison of model mechanisms across different scenarios, as 
opposed to deep learning methods such as CNNs which are relatively untested in SAR damage mapping and offer limited model 
transparency.
A Scikit-Learn implementation of the Random Forest Classifier [ 52 ] was utilised, with 100 estimators and a minimum number of 
samples per leaf of 5 to limit model overfitting. A classification threshold of 0.5 was used. The same machine learning model pa -
rameters were used for each testing scenario, such that model performance differences would be solely due to the different train -
ing/testing split scenarios themselves.
Due to the significant imbalance of ‘damaged ’ and ‘un-damaged ’ classes across disaster scenes, the class weight parameter was set 
to ‘balanced ’ . This means that the weights applied to each sample used for training the model were inversely proportional to the class 
frequency in the training dataset [ 52 ].

5. Discussion
This study evaluates the transferability of medium-resolution SAR ML Models to map building damage following natural disasters. 
By utilising three testing scenarios, model adaptability under different combinations of disaster types and geographic contexts was able 
to be tested, enabling an understanding of the model performance in real-world scenarios. Specifically, the objectives were to 
determine the extent of decline in performance when models were required to generalise to unseen disaster events in varying contexts 
and evaluate whether a disaster-type specific model outperformed a general disaster-agnostic model.
The application of a realistic transferability testing scheme in the global testing scenario reduced model performance, substantially 
lowering median Macro F1 Score, PR-AUC and OA as compared to the reference scenario by 20.6%, 42.9% and 7.5% respectively. This 
suggests that previous studies which did not test for transferability may have been overly optimistic in their reported performances. 
This could be due to similar issues described by Ploton et al. [ 42 ], in that training and test sets in the reference scenario were not 
spatially segmented, and rather than finding the characteristics of damage itself, algorithm predictive power was due to local spatial 
autocorrelation. The performance drop between testing scenarios aligned broadly to previous studies using higher resolution SAR 
intensity which evaluated transferability [ 24–26 ]. These previous studies generally evaluated local model transferability on the same 
disaster events or disaster types within similar geographic regions. The requirement for the global scenario algorithm to adapt to 
differing building characteristics, geographical and environmental contexts globally likely reduced the model ability to classify 
damage, as-well as the differing characteristics and severity of individual disaster events and specific disaster types.
To contextualise these transferability findings, our reference model testing scenario performance was comparable to previous 
coherence and intensity-based machine learning studies. The best performing disaster event in the reference scenario, the Santa Rosa 
fire (F1 score of 0.90), was comparable to the performance achieved by Xie et al. [ 23 ], who, focused on the Camp Fire in the same U.S. 
state and year as the Santa Rosa fire and achieved an F1 score of 0.93 when focusing on residential buildings, although only 0.77 on 
commercial buildings. Other variations in performance were consistent with results reported by Rao et al. [ 38 ], where the effects of 
class imbalance likely contributed to instances of near-random model performance, while more balanced classifications achieved 
respectable Macro F1 scores, ranging from 0.47 to 0.79. Our reference scenario model exceeded performance of Akhmadiya et al. [ 21 ] 
(OA of 77%) and Karimzadeh and Mastuoka [ 22 ] (OA of 84%) for the Amatrice Earthquake in both the reference and global scenarios 
(OA of 94% and 87% respectively), although we acknowledge the study area extent and damage type classification were not identical 
between these studies.
In this study, a wildfire specific model outperformed a disaster-agnostic model. This suggests that the characteristics of damage as 
viewed by a SAR sensor differs between wildfires and the other disaster types assessed. This is not unexpected, as different disaster 
types are expected to result in different impacts on infrastructure. Under the wildfire-specific scenario, a few features accounted for the

The inconsistent model performance for building damage classification can potentially be explained by the spatial resolution of the 
Sentinel-1 sensor under Interferometric Wide-Swath mode. As shown in Figures A1, A2 and S4 to S14, the most successful model 
performance often occurred when building damage was spatially clustered together. In contrast, for Hurricane Michael, the Mexico

predictions may therefore reflect neighbourhood-scale change rather than isolated building-level damage. Small, isolated pockets of 
damage are therefore more likely to be missed by the model, which could delay disaster response and result in misunderstanding of 
damage distribution. Use of higher resolution sensors which capture the phase information needed for interferometric coherence 
calculation, such TerraSAR-X/Tandem-X or the commercial ICEYE or Capella sensors, would therefore likely be able to overcome this 
limitation.
A particular example of poor model performance was the St Martin Hurricane, which was an outlier in terms of low accuracy in the 
reference scenario, and suffered the lowest Macro F1 Score in the global scenario (0.40). This low performance could be due to the 
unique characteristics of the geography of St Martin Island, being the only island studied, and featuring a uniquely high rate of damage 
(38%). Performance was also low in both the reference and global transferability scenario for Hurricane Michael, the only other 
hurricane event reviewed in this study, indicating that the damage caused by storms may be difficult to map using SAR due to the 
characteristics of storm-induced damage. Additionally, label uncertainty in the ground truth data, particularly in the case of the 
Copernicus EMS dataset (reported OA of 85%), may have contributed to reduced performance in some cases.
Several key limitations constrain the interpretation of this studies findings. Beyond the technical limitations of Sentinel-1 relating 
to noise and resolution of damage at the individual building level, there are limitations relating to study design worthy of note. The 
limited sample size in terms of the number of events (n = 11) studied remains relatively limited and does not capture global geographic 
and disaster characteristic variability, and as the ground-truth datasets are from two sources where the definition of “ damage ” varies 
slightly, and the interpretation or application of these damage definitions may vary across disaster types, the comparisons and gen -
eralisations across events should be interpreted with caution. The reported performance metrics are dependent on the selected 
threshold, and model outputs correspond to uncalibrated RF class scores. These model output scores should not be interpreted as 
probabilities, and would require calibration before being suitable for direct operational decision-making. Further, while the use of a 
Random Forest with manually extracted SAR features acts as a baseline through which transferability can be assessed, whether the 
observed transferability limitations and associated drop in performance extends to more complex model types to the same extent 
remains to be tested.
A limitation of this study and previous research into coherence and intensity based damage mapping is the length of the timeseries 
utilised as a baseline, utilising three images, generally covering 18 or 32 days over time. Dietrich et al. [ 59 ], utilising intensity data 
from a three-month time series to map damage from the war in Ukraine, when testing transferability to other regions, achieved a macro 
average F1 score of 0.71. Incorporating a longer time-series of coherence into model predictions as-well as intensity would establish a 
better baseline of natural variation and therefore could improve model performance, however this may lead to longer processing time 
and therefore delayed disaster response. A cost-benefit analysis, investigating the performance improvement and processing delays 
associated with a longer baseline would demonstrate whether the inclusion of the longer baseline is worthwhile.
Another improvement could be the inclusion of more features for model prediction. Backscattering characteristics are not only 
affected by the shape and roughness of the target, but also by the dielectric properties, heavily influenced by factors such as soil 
moisture [ 60 ], with soil moisture being mapped using Sentinel-1 backscattering [ 61 ]. Damage mapping algorithms could likely be 
improved by incorporating soil moisture data, especially when transferring to regions with different environmental characteristics. 
Given coherence's sensitivity to vegetation [ 15 ], inclusion of Normalised-Difference-Vegetation-Index products could potentially also 
boost classifier results. Other information, such as the number of stories in the building, tested by Rao et al. [ 38 ], could also improve 
performance.
Deep learning algorithms are gaining popularity as alternatives to traditional machine learning methods, with CNNs consistently 
outperforming conventional algorithms [ 62 ]. The primary advantage of CNNs is automatic feature extraction, where the CNN auto -
matically extracts the most important features in the input images [ 63 ]. Rather than individual pixel values as the input, the CNN takes 
an array of pixels and considers the contextual information in the image to produce a result [ 64 ]. This could be valuable for damage 
detection using SAR imagery, as key texture features manually extracted from SAR images rely on contextual spatial information. SAR 
interferometry has been relatively unexplored with CNNs [ 18 ]. While there have been a few efforts using SAR coherence and intensity 
for flood mapping [ 65 , 66 ], only one study has applied inSAR with CNNs for damage detection. Xie et al. [ 23 ] tested a CNN on the 2018 
Camp Fire, where it was only slightly outperformed by a GBM. A comparative study that evaluates out-of-domain model performance, 
leveraging a larger and more diverse dataset, should therefore be conducted to test the applicability for CNNs for medium resolution 
SAR damage mapping.

6. Conclusions
This study evaluated the transferability of coherence and intensity based SAR building damage mapping using machine learning, 
achieved by using a large ground truth dataset of 205,731 buildings from 11 different disaster events. To date, there is little published 
on this topic in the literature. This study revealed several significant insights regarding model performance and transferability.
This study established that when Random Forest models were applied to unseen disaster events across diverse geographic, envi -
ronmental and urban contexts as-well as various disaster types, there was a notable decline in performance. This finding suggests that 
earlier assessments of SAR machine learning model performance may have been overly optimistic in studies that did not rigorously 
evaluate model transferability. While whether a similar performance drop extends to more complex model classes such as Deep 
Learning architectures with learned feature representations remains an open question without direct comparative evidence, the 
observed performance drop highlights the necessity of testing algorithms beyond their training contexts to ensure reliability in real- 
world applications.
In addition, this study found that a model trained on one specific disaster type outperformed a disaster-agnostic model in the case of 
wildfires. This indicates that the building damage characteristics of wildfires differ from the other assessed disaster types as seen by the 
SAR sensor, and focusing the model on one disaster type can outweigh the value of extra training data from other disaster types, 
however this has yet to be tested beyond wildfires.
Overall, the performance of the algorithms was too inconsistent and insufficient for effective building-level damage mapping for 
disaster response. However, there were positive outcomes for several disaster events, and the performance in the wildfire-specific 
scenario showed considerable promise. Future research should focus on expanding the number of disaster events utilised to ensure 
robustness in results, and further testing of context-specific algorithms focused on specific disaster types and geographic regions. 
Further, the effectiveness of deep learning algorithms as well as the inclusion of longer SAR time series and ancillary data sources 
should be evaluated and tested to improve model performance.
In conclusion, while this study has highlighted critical challenges and opportunities in SAR-based building damage mapping, it also 
emphasizes the urgent need for further research. Given the increasing frequency and severity of natural disasters [ 67 ], enhancing the 
accuracy and timeliness of damage assessment methods is critical for improving disaster response and recovery efforts across the globe.
